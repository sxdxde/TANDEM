1 · Abstract

KV-Tier simulates a two-tier KV cache for vision-language model decoding. The HOT tier holds entries in FP16 on conventional DRAM or HBM. The WARM tier is a compute-in-memory tier that holds entries in INT4: about ten times cheaper per byte in energy, about one and a half times slower, and lossy. We built a SimPy simulator, a synthetic attention trace, and a dynamic policy that moves entries between tiers from a live importance estimate. Every move is charged.

On the synthetic trace the dynamic policy buys about 49% less quality loss for about 55% more energy than a fixed split by modality. On real attention traces from SmolVLM-256M that trade-off does not appear. Visual tokens receive about 6% of attention, the fixed split is already near the quality floor, and thresholds picked on two traces failed the no-worse-quality check on a third. A sixth round (M6) tested per-layer entries and sparse reads. Per-layer entries with dense reads give the first real-trace dynamic win, about 20% less energy at roughly neutral quality, with a marginal +0.8% held-out quality miss of the strict rule. With sparse reads the win disappears. M6 also measured the INT4 error at about 0.09, against the 0.05 we assumed. That scales absolute quality values by about 1.8× and leaves every ratio unchanged. This page records each step, including the dead ends and one post-hoc choice.

Motivation

A vision-language model decoding an answer reads a KV cache dominated by visual tokens. In our real traces 832 of 876 prompt positions are image patches. Most of them are almost never used: the median visual token receives 0.02× the attention of a uniform spread. A small set is used constantly, and that set stays stable across decoding.

A tiered memory can exploit that skew. Keep the few important entries in precise, fast memory and park the rest in a cheap compute-in-memory tier. The open question is placement. A fixed rule by modality is free. A dynamic rule can follow actual attention but pays for every move. This project asks whether the dynamic rule earns its cost. It responds to published KV-cache work such as HybridKV, ZipVL, AirCache and VL-Cache.

2 · System

A trace supplies prefill entries and one attention distribution per decode step. The simulator replays it against a placement policy, charges each access to the tier the entry lives in, and records metrics per step. Traces are either synthetic or taken from a real model; the simulator does not know which.

3 · Cost model and metrics

Each tier charges a fixed overhead plus a cost linear in the entry's effective size in that tier. An entry's size is stored at FP16 and rescaled by the tier's bytes per element, so WARM both moves fewer bytes and pays less per byte.

QuantityDefinition
Access latencybase_ns + ns_per_byte × effective_bytes
Access energybase_pJ + pJ_per_byte × effective_bytes
Move cost10 × one HOT access, latency and energy, either direction
Total latencySum of per-access latencies, fully serialized. A total-work upper bound, not wall-clock.
Quality loss (per step)Σ attention_weight × tier.quant_error
Mean quality lossPer-step quality loss averaged over decode steps. Zero for flat, 0.05 for all-WARM.
WARM attention massFraction of each step's attention weight served from WARM, averaged over steps.
Efficiency (M4b)−quality change % ÷ energy change %, both vs static. A heuristic, not an objective.
Assumed, then measured: the WARM quantization error is set to 0.05 in the config. It began as an order-of-magnitude guess. M6 measured about 0.09 on SmolVLM's KV cache (section 6). Quality loss is linear in the error, so absolute quality values on this page are about 1.8× too small. Every ratio against static is unaffected. The config was left at 0.05 so committed results reproduce.

Other assumptions

  • Every active entry is read once per decode step, regardless of its attention weight. Real systems skip low-attention tokens. M6 adds an optional sparse mode that reads only the top 10% by importance.
  • Quality is a reported axis. It is not folded into a scalar cost, and policies cannot see it.
  • The WARM energy ratio of 0.1 and latency ratio of 1.5 are named constants chosen to match commonly cited compute-in-memory trade-offs, not fitted to hardware.
  • One KV entry per token by default. Real caches are per layer and per head. M5 traces average attention over 30 layers and 9 heads; M6 adds per-layer entries, still averaged over heads.
  • Synthetic trace: 20 text and 500 visual tokens, 64 decode steps, 8% of visual tokens salient, seed 0 unless a sweep says otherwise.

4 · Method and findings, in order

Each entry says what we did, what we saw, and whether it helped. Green marks a result that held up, red marks a result that went against us, amber marks a result that changed nothing. Numbers are from NOTES.md and the CSVs in results/.

M1▲ held

Synthetic trace and tier cost model

  • Built a prefill-plus-decode trace where attention mixes recency with a small fixed set of salient visual tokens, then fixed the HOT and WARM cost constants.
  • The validation plot showed salient visual tokens settling near an importance of 0.0088 and average text near 0.005 to 0.006. The first policy thresholds (promote 0.006, demote 0.002) came from that plot.
M2▲ held

SimPy simulator and two static baselines

  • Flat (everything HOT) and static modality (text HOT, visual WARM).
  • Static modality cuts energy 88.2% against flat (72.6M pJ to 8.55M pJ) and costs 33.7% more latency (797.7k ns to 1066.5k ns).
M3▼ lost

Dynamic hybrid policy with move cost

  • Entries start at their modality tier and move on a live importance EMA with a hysteresis band.
  • It lost to static: +67% energy and +2% latency, with 82 promotions and 64 demotions.
  • Cause: entries near the prefill/decode boundary get a high early weight from position alone, get promoted, then demoted as recency fades. Each round trip pays the move cost.
M4 · step 1▼ model was degenerate

Sensitivity sweep on energy and latency only

  • 174 configurations: promote and demote thresholds crossed with six move-cost multipliers.
  • 87 of 174 beat static on energy, but the best ones had zero or one promotion. They only demoted text to WARM.
  • A trivial all-WARM policy reached −78.6% energy and +2.6% latency against static, beating every dynamic configuration.
  • Cause: nothing penalised INT4, and WARM has unlimited capacity, so HOT had no reason to exist. The comparison could not say anything about placement.
  • Decision: add a quality penalty for reading from WARM (the user chose this over a HOT capacity limit).
M4 · step 2▲ held (synthetic only)

Quality penalty added

  • Each tier carries a quant_error. The simulator accrues attention-weighted quality loss and WARM attention mass.
  • All-WARM now has the worst quality (0.050, against 0.034 for static and 0 for flat), so the degeneracy is gone.
  • 24 of 174 configurations beat static on both energy and quality, but quality improved only 0.27%. The energy win came from demoting low-attention text, which says more about the synthetic trace than about the policy.
  • The real trade-off was on the other side. Promoting salient visual tokens cut quality loss by up to 56%. At thresholds 0.006 and 0.002 the undamped policy cost +35.8% / +67.3% / +102.4% energy at 1× / 10× / 20× move cost, for −44.6% quality loss at every cost.
  • Move cost shifts only the energy side. It does not change what promotion buys.
M4 · step 3● diagnosed

Comparison bars and tier timeline

  • The timeline exposed a burst of 21 demotions at step 0. Text entries start HOT with an EMA of zero, which is below the demote threshold, so all 20 prefill text tokens plus one more were demoted at once, each paying 10× move cost.
  • Steps 4 to 11 held about 50 promotions as salient visual tokens crossed the threshold. That burst was useful work: it took the WARM attention share from about 97% to about 36%.
  • After that, a trickle of 0 to 3 moves per step, mostly boundary entries.
M4b● partial fix

Damping the dynamic policy

  • Added warmup_steps, min_dwell_steps and damp_promotions, all off by default. Swept 6 warmups × 6 dwells × both promotion modes, averaged over 5 trace seeds.
  • Warmup 2 beats undamped on both axes: energy +55.8% against +64.8%, quality loss −49.3% against −46.1%, reconfiguration share of energy 13.0% against 19.1%, tier moves 85 against 131. It became the new default.
  • Post-hoc choice. Before the sweep I fixed the rule "lowest energy subject to at least 20% quality gain". It drifted to the edge of the quality budget and picked dwell 20, which gives away 11 points of quality gain to do less. After seeing that I switched to quality-gain-per-energy. The new rule favoured warmup 2, and I adopted it. This was decided after the results.
  • The burst only moved. Warmup 2 shifts the 20 text demotions from step 0 to step 1. At a decay of 0.9 the EMA needs about 20 steps to converge, so text still looks unimportant after two updates. In this trace those demotions are mostly correct, just early.
  • Dwell 20 removes steady-state churn but batches every move into one burst at step 19. For the first 20 steps 70% to 97% of attention is served from WARM.
  • Bias-corrected EMA was neutral. It gave +78.8% energy and −61.1% quality loss (median over a grid) against +55.9% and −49.3% for the raw EMA. That is a different operating point, not a better frontier, and the thresholds were calibrated on the raw EMA. Left off by default.
  • The remaining +55% energy is mostly the inherent cost of reading promoted tokens from HOT. Move cost is only 13% of dynamic energy.
  • Thresholds matter more than damping. The best efficiency in the threshold sweep is promote 0.008 with demote 0.001 (+35% energy for −38% quality loss). We did not adopt it because the right trade depends on how energy and quality are weighed.
M5▼ did not replicate

Real attention traces from SmolVLM-256M

  • Ran the model on three locally generated images (a bar chart, a scene, a boxed word on noise), greedy-decoded 64 tokens, and recorded each step's attention averaged over 30 layers and 9 heads. The model answered all three correctly, so it was using the image.
  • The premise holds. The top 8% of visual tokens carry 61% to 70% of visual attention, and 74% to 83% of that set is the same at the first and last step.
  • The magnitude does not. Visual tokens get 5% to 7% of attention. About 35% goes to the attention sink at position 0 and about 36% to generated tokens. The synthetic trace had 68% of attention served from WARM under static modality; the real traces have about 6%.
  • Thresholds calibrated on synthetic data never promote anything on real data: uniform attention is about 0.0011 and the 99th-percentile visual EMA is about 0.0008, below the 0.006 promote threshold. With them, dynamic only demotes text: −23% to −27% energy and +37% to +42% quality loss. Thresholds were re-expressed as multiples of uniform attention.
  • Protocol fixed before looking: choose thresholds on chart and scene (lowest energy with quality loss no worse than static), test on the held-out noise_label trace. Chosen: promote 2.0× and demote 0.4× uniform. Training mean: −6.6% energy, −2.5% quality loss.
  • Held-out result: −14.4% energy but +11.9% quality loss (0.0027 to 0.0031). The no-worse-quality criterion failed. Per trace the training results were also mixed: chart +5.1%, scene −10.0%.
  • The stakes are small. Static modality's quality loss is about 0.003 against a worst case of 0.05, and flat to static is −88% to −90% energy. Dynamic moves energy by about ±10% around that.
  • A per-layer check (chart trace, 16 steps) shows the layer average hides structure. Visual attention ranges from about 3% to 39% by layer, up to 70% in one head of layer 0, and the sink is near zero in layers 0, 1 and 11 and 40% to 78% in layers 12 to 29.
M6● conditional

Per-layer entries, sparse reads and a measured INT4 error

  • Added one entry per token and layer, a sparse-read mode (top 10% by live importance plus new entries), and a measurement of INT4 error on SmolVLM's KV cache. Same selection protocol as M5, 408 simulations.
  • Per-layer entries with dense reads gave the first real-trace dynamic win: about −20% energy on all three traces, held-out quality +0.8%. That narrowly misses the strict no-worse rule, and we report it as a miss.
  • With sparse reads the win disappears (+3.7% to +7.5% energy). With layer-averaged entries and sparse reads the policy never moves anything.
  • Sparse reads cut the flat-over-static energy ratio from about 9.3× to 1.6× to 2.0×, so tiering is worth much less when a system skips low-attention tokens.
  • Measured INT4 error is about 0.09, against 0.05 assumed. Ratios are unaffected; absolute quality values scale by about 1.8×.
  • The likely mechanism is demotion of text entries in layers that ignore them. This is an inference from totals, not an instrumented result.

Ledger of negative and neutral results

  • ▼The first sweep's headline result (87 of 174 configurations beating static) was an artifact of a cost model with no quality term.
  • ▼The 24 configurations that beat static on both axes in M4 did so by demoting text. They are not evidence for dynamic promotion.
  • ●The damping default was chosen after the results, by a different rule than the one fixed in advance.
  • ●Warmup 2 moved the step-0 demotion burst to step 1 instead of removing it.
  • ●A bias-corrected EMA gave a different operating point, not a better one.
  • ▼On real traces the synthetic trade-off did not replicate, and the held-out check failed.
  • ▼With sparse reads the per-layer dynamic win disappears or reverses, and layer-averaged entries leave the policy nothing to do.
  • ●The per-layer dense result misses the strict no-worse-quality rule by +0.8% on the held-out trace.
  • ●The mechanism behind the per-layer gain is inferred from totals, not instrumented.
  • ▼The assumed INT4 error was about half the measured value. Ratios survive, absolute quality numbers do not.
  • ●One process slip: a test with a missing import was committed, then fixed in the next commit (748ec98).

A note on which numbers come from where. The narrative figures for M4 step 2 (−44.6%, +67.3%) come from the undamped policy. The CSVs and charts below were regenerated with the warmup 2 default, so the same thresholds now read +55.4% energy and −48.8% quality loss at 10× move cost.

6 · M6: per-layer entries, sparse reads, measured INT4 error

M5 left three open questions. Does averaging over layers hide a placement win? Does the dynamic policy still matter if a system reads only the entries that matter? And how wrong is the assumed INT4 error? M6 answers each. The protocol is M5's, fixed in advance: thresholds in multiples of uniform attention, chosen on chart and scene as the lowest energy with mean quality no worse than static, then tested on the held-out noise_label trace. The run is 408 simulations (results/m6_sweep.csv).

What we built

  • Sparse access. Step 0 is dense. After that only the top 10% of active entries by live importance EMA, plus entries created in the last step, are read. Skipped attention mass is reported and costs no energy. It does not depend on placement (tested).
  • Assumption: the importance estimate sees the true attention weights at zero cost. That is an upper bound on what a real approximate scorer could do.
  • Per-layer entries. One entry per token and layer. Each layer's attention is renormalised and divided by the number of layers, so a step's mass still sums to 1. Entry size matches a single-entry token, so absolute energies are about 30× the layer-averaged ones. Compare ratios only.
  • Quality is linear in attention weights, so static policies score identically in both views (0.0031 on chart). Per-layer entries change only what the dynamic policy can do.

Strict rule, marginal miss. On the held-out trace the per-layer dense configuration saves 20.0% energy and loses 0.8% on quality against static. The rule fixed in advance was quality no worse than static, so this misses by 0.8%. We report it as a miss and do not round it to zero. Quality was −2.0% and −10.3% on the two training traces.

Mechanism: arithmetic inference, not instrumented. In the static per-layer baseline the roughly 1.3k HOT text entries (44 tokens × 30 layers) cost about 170M of about 380M pJ. The dynamic policy saves about 78M pJ, roughly half of that. The WARM share of attention barely moves (6.2% to 6.1%). From these totals, the saving comes mostly from demoting text entries in layers where text is unimportant, plus a few promotions of visual entries in layers that attend to them. It does not come mainly from rescuing salient visual tokens. This is the same text-demotion channel seen on the synthetic trace. We did not break the saving down per entry to confirm it.

Measured INT4 error

We quantized SmolVLM's prefill K and V per token to INT4 (min-max) and measured relative L2 error, over 30 layers and 3 images (results/int4_error.csv, 360 rows). At group size 32 the error is 0.102 for K and 0.081 for V. At group size 64 it is 0.134 and 0.094. Visual and text tokens agree within about 1%.

The assumed 0.05 was about half the measured value of roughly 0.09. We left the config at 0.05. Quality loss is the sum of attention weight times error, so it is linear in the error and the same factor applies to every policy. Every ratio against static is unchanged, and so are the threshold choices, since the selection rule compares ratios. Only absolute quality values scale, by about 1.8×. Read any absolute quality number in this report that way. Keeping 0.05 means the committed results still reproduce.

Two limits. We did not compute a per-channel variant for K, because a guard skipped it for token counts that are not a multiple of 64. And relative L2 error stands in for quality loss here, the same proxy the rest of the report uses.

8 · Limits and next steps

Limits

  • The real-trace evidence is thin: one 256M model, three generated images, 64 decode steps, selection on two traces and one held-out trace. Natural photos, documents and larger models are untested.
  • The synthetic M4 conclusions describe the synthetic trace. They should not be read as properties of vision-language models.
  • The config still carries the assumed 0.05 INT4 error. Measured error is about 0.09, so absolute quality values are about 1.8× too small. Ratios against static are unaffected. The per-channel K variant was not measured.
  • Latency is serialized total work, not wall-clock time.
  • Per-layer entries average over heads within a layer. Per-head structure is still hidden.
  • Sparse reads assume a perfect importance estimate at zero cost, which is an upper bound for any real scorer. The 50%, 20% and 5% top-k numbers come from an unsaved ad-hoc run.
  • The per-layer dense gain needs about a thousand tier moves per session, and it narrowly misses the strict quality rule.
  • The mechanism behind the per-layer gain is inferred from totals, not instrumented.
  • No quality-aware policy exists yet. The policy never sees quality.
  • The planned oracle comparison against ground-truth top-k attended tokens is not done.

Next steps

  • A layer-aware static prior (modality × layer) as a stronger baseline than static modality. The per-layer dynamic win looks like a layer effect, so this baseline may capture it without any moves.
  • Per-head entries.
  • A real approximate importance estimator for sparse reads, in place of true weights.
  • Sweep move cost and top-k fraction on real traces.
  • Natural images, documents and a larger vision-language model.
  • The oracle top-k comparison from the M5 plan, and a quality-aware promotion rule.

9 · Conclusion

A fixed split by modality does most of the work. On real traces it cuts energy by about 90% against keeping everything HOT, for a quality loss near 0.003, and sparse reads shrink the remaining gap between flat and static from about 9.3× to under 2×.

The dynamic policy's synthetic trade-off (about −49% quality loss for about +55% energy) did not replicate. The synthetic trace sent about 68% of attention to WARM, and real traces send about 6%. On real traces its value is conditional. With per-layer entries and dense reads it saves about 20% energy at roughly neutral quality, narrowly missing the strict rule, and likely by demoting text entries rather than rescuing visual ones. With sparse reads that benefit vanishes, and it needs about a thousand moves per session.

The INT4 error we assumed was about half the measured value. That does not change any ratio. The next experiment worth running is a layer-aware static prior, since it would show whether the dynamic policy's remaining gain needs dynamics at all.
